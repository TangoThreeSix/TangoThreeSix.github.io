### Iran War — Escalation, Retaliation, and an Uncertain Equilibrium

The first week of September brought the most significant US-Iran exchange since the 60-day Islamabad MoU expired on August 17. On September 1, Iran struck US personnel in Jordan with approximately 25 ballistic missiles, per US officials speaking to Axios. Only about half reached Jordanian airspace — Jordanian defenses intercepted 10, and three landed in open areas. That roughly 50% failure rate closely mirrors Iran's April 2024 True Promise I attack on Israel, suggesting degradation of Iran's longer-range missile stocks rather than a one-off technical glitch. ISW assessed the failure rate may indicate Iran is relying on older or hastily produced missiles with lower reliability.

The US response was immediate. CENTCOM confirmed September 1 strikes targeting Iranian air defense sites, radar systems, maritime assets, mine-laying capabilities, and communications infrastructure. A second wave followed on September 2-3. Trump stated Iran had nearly rebuilt some radar systems before the US struck them again — a pattern of reconstitution-and-restrike that has defined the post-ceasefire phase.

**The Strait balance shifted — briefly.** On August 31 and September 1, approximately 40 vessels carrying millions of barrels transited the Strait of Hormuz — the highest volume since the war began, and close to pre-war averages of 21.6 million barrels per day in Q4 2025. Energy Secretary Wright confirmed 17 million barrels moved on August 31 alone. But the window was narrow: by September 2, only four commercial vessels transited, per Kpler shipping data, as fears of further escalation spiked. The pattern — a surge of traffic when the US asserts control, followed by a freeze when Iran threatens — suggests neither side can sustain dominance of the waterway, but both can disrupt it at will.

**New escalation vectors.** The Financial Times reported on September 1 that Russia has been covertly helping Iran develop advanced cruise missile technology since approximately November 2023. Separately, an unnamed US source told Al Arabiya that the September 1 strikes also sought to preempt Iranian attacks on undersea communications cables in the Strait. IRGC-affiliated media floated the cable threat as early as May, and the WSJ reported in August that the IRGC had developed plans to sabotage undersea internet cables. It's unclear whether Iran possesses the technical capability to damage them — but the threat itself is a signal that Tehran is looking beyond vessel attacks for leverage.

**The economic vise tightens.** Iran's rial traded at 220,000 tomans to the dollar this week, per Iran International — a figure that government planners' own 2021 projections said would not be reached until 2027 under a continued-sanctions scenario. IMF's April outlook projected Iran's economy would contract 6.1% in 2026 with inflation at 68.9%. Iran exported zero crude oil in May 2026, down from 2.1 million barrels per day pre-war. The US declared an "Economic D-Day" in late August, adding fresh sanctions on top of an already comprehensive blockade.

**Iraqi militia chess.** Iran is pursuing two parallel lines in Iraq: pushing for a settlement where Iranian-backed militias "discreetly" retain weapons in PMF-controlled depots, while simultaneously building a new cadre of smaller, more ideological cells under tighter IRGC control, per ISW reporting. The playbook is familiar — formal disarmament on paper, operational capacity preserved in practice.

### Russian Drone Warfare — The Jet Shahed Goes Mainstream

If August was the month jet-powered drones became the primary threat, September is shaping up to be when they become the dominant one. Ukraine Air Force spokesman Yurii Ihnat reported approximately 2,800 jet-powered Geran launches in August — nearly double the ~1,500 in July and a fivefold increase over June. Jet variants now account for up to two-thirds of Russia's long-range attack sorties in some waves. Ukraine's interception rate against propeller Shaheds remains above 90%, but falls to roughly 60% against the faster jet models per the same spokesman.

**Production scaling.** Ukrainian military intelligence (DIU) assessed in August that Russian industry produces roughly 3,000 jet-powered Geran-4/5 airframes per month, alongside approximately 2,800 piston Geran-2s — meaning jet output has overtaken piston production. Separately, RBC-Ukraine cited military sources estimating Russia plans to produce 2,000 jet Gerans in September specifically, with roughly 160 attack drones allocated to strike units daily. The engine bottleneck is significant — jet drone production depends heavily on Chinese component suppliers, per the same reporting.

The tactical shift is straightforward: at speeds exceeding 350 km/h for the Geran-4 (and reportedly up to 600 km/h for the Geran-5, per HUR assessments), jet drones outrun most of Ukraine's propeller-driven interceptor fleet. A Geran-4 destroyed a Ukrainian MiG-29 at a southern airfield during pre-mission preparation on June 27, and another struck the locomotive of a moving passenger train in Odesa Oblast on August 13, per the drone-warfare.com research database. These are target sets a 185 km/h Shahed-136 could never have held.

**Civilian toll rising.** A Russian drone strike hit an ammunition depot in Myla on August 29, killing 38 people. Twelve more were killed in strikes across Ukraine on September 1, per Al Jazeera reporting. The jet Shahed's speed and accuracy have made populated areas harder to defend.

**Ground war in slow motion.** Russia seized roughly 90 square kilometers in August, per ISW open-source assessments — less than a fifth of August 2025's territorial gains and just 60 square kilometers over the entire summer (June-August), compared with 1,440 square kilometers in summer 2025. Russian casualties remain extraordinarily high: Ukraine reports 42,020 in August, slightly below July's 42,860. Recruitment rates are estimated at roughly 32,500 per month, per Ukrainian intelligence — meaning Russia is consistently losing more troops than it can replace. Zelensky has publicly shared intelligence suggesting Putin will conscript 300,000 or more troops in October.

Ukrainian commander-in-chief Mykhailo Drapatyi reinforced flanks around Sloviansk and Dobropillia but kept the Kostiantynivka front largely unchanged — a pattern consistent with fighting a prolonged urban rearguard to inflict attrition, as Ukraine did in Bakhmut and Chasiv Yar.

### Ukrainian Drone Innovation — Deep Strike Meets Drone-on-Drone Defense

Ukraine's long-range strike campaign continued to evolve from refinery-hunting to something more strategically coherent. The Unmanned Systems Forces destroyed 33 Russian air defense elements in August, bringing the 2026 total to 263 — a campaign aimed at opening corridors for deeper strikes. Twelve refineries, two gas-processing plants, and three oil terminals were hit in August, per Ukraine's defense ministry. During this past week, Ukraine struck the Yanos refinery in Yaroslavl and the Kirishi and Ust-Luga refineries near Leningrad — all over 800 kilometers from the border.

**The oil numbers are starting to tell.** Russian Black Sea oil exports from Novorossiysk fell from 800,000 barrels per day in July to 350,000 barrels per day in August, per Ukraine's Foreign Intelligence Service — though this figure should be treated as an intelligence estimate rather than independently confirmed trade data. Russia extended its diesel export ban to the end of September and its gasoline export ban to January 2027. Crimea is experiencing fuel rationing, with distribution restricted to government vehicles.

**Sea drones go expeditionary.** The Katran X1.2 unmanned surface vessel — capable of carrying up to 27 interceptor drones and with a stated operational range exceeding 1,500 kilometers — was demonstrated on the Dnipro River and confirmed in combat operations, per Ukrainian media and the manufacturer. The Magura V5, which has been included in a US manufacturing joint venture, continues to demonstrate that a country without a conventional navy can deny sea control to a major power. The US Defense Innovation Unit opened its SWAP-USV competition at the end of July with $100 million in prize money and a potential $200 million procurement budget — a direct acknowledgment that Ukraine's combat-tested USV designs are shaping Pentagon acquisition.

**The interceptor economics keep improving.** SkyFall, a Ukrainian manufacturer, reports its P1-SUN fiber-optic interceptor costs $1,000 per unit and has downed over 1,500 Shaheds and 1,000 other drones — though these are manufacturer claims without independent verification. Wild Hornets reports its Sting interceptor at $2,500 has downed 3,900 drones since May 2025, including the first confirmed jet-powered Geran-3. A two-person crew set a record in March, downing 23 Shaheds in a single engagement using Sting interceptors. At $1,000-2,500 per interceptor versus $35,000+ per Shahed (CSIS estimate), the cost-exchange ratio has flipped decisively in Ukraine's favor — but only against the propeller variants. Jet Shaheds, at 350-600 km/h, have pushed the intercept problem back toward missile-based solutions.

**Production at scale.** Ukraine's Deputy Defense Minister Serhii Boiev stated early this year that Ukraine has the capacity to produce more than 7 million drones in 2026 — approximately quadruple 2024 output. Russia plans 7.3 million FPV drones of its own, per Commander-in-Chief Syrsky. The drone war has become an industrial production race as much as a tactical one.

### Connecting the Threads

Three cross-theater developments deserve attention.

First, **Russia is helping Iran close its cruise missile gap.** The FT's September 1 report on covert Russian cruise missile technology transfers to Iran since late 2023, if accurate, means the same Russian defense industry that's producing 3,000 jet Gerans monthly is also working to give Iran a capability it notably lacked in the opening months of the war. The timeline matters: supersonic cruise missile development takes years, and it's unclear how far along Iran is. But the pipeline is real.

Second, **the US munitions burn rate is now a structural problem, not a cyclical one.** CSIS estimates Patriot stocks are below 1,000 interceptors (~35% of pre-war), THAAD stocks at approximately 250, and over 1,000 Tomahawks expended. The FY27 budget requests 2,798 PAC-3 MSE missiles (up from ~650 annual production) and 857 THAAD interceptors — but deliveries won't begin meaningfully until 2029-2030. The Lockheed Martin Patriot contract awarded in July is worth up to $58.6 billion through 2032. The problem isn't money — it's industrial throughput, and that takes years, not appropriations cycles, to solve. The zero-carrier presence in the Western Pacific while USS George Washington operates in CENTCOM is a concrete manifestation: fighting one war means accepting risk in another theater.

Third, **the tactical innovation cycle is compressing.** Ukraine went from taming the piston Shahed with mobile machine-gun teams and quadcopter interceptors to facing jet-powered variants within roughly 12 months. The Alexa Spatium jet interceptor entered service within weeks of the Geran surge. Russia is now fitting Shaheds with rear-mounted jammers targeting interceptor command links — and Ukraine is responding with fiber-optic and AI-guided alternatives. The window of advantage for any single system is now measured in months, not years. The country that fields the fastest adaptation cycle wins — and both sides are adapting faster than their adversaries' acquisition systems were designed to handle.

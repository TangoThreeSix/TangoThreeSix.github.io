### Iran War — A War Now Shaped by a Calendar

The week's most consequential statement wasn't a strike. On September 9, Trump told reporters the war would end "immediately after" the November midterms, because Iran "can't hold out any longer," adding that negotiations "could possibly happen, but it's not something we're looking at." He repeated it September 12. Whether that's strategy or positioning is unknowable, but the asymmetry is real: Trump has midterms, Netanyahu has an October election, and Iran — with no legislative elections until 2028 — has neither.

The kinetic week was real. On September 8-9, Iran fired roughly 20 ballistic missiles, some with cluster munitions, at Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan; Jordan's armed forces said they intercepted 18. A single US official told CBS/Reuters that roughly 30 Patriot interceptors were expended defending the base — a striking cost ratio for one salvo. An A-10 reportedly lost a wing and about eight F-15s took light damage; treat those details as reported by anonymous officials, not confirmed.

At sea, CENTCOM confirmed US forces destroyed five IRGC tankers on September 8, bringing the total to ten since September 5. On September 6 the IRGC claimed missiles had struck a US carrier and destroyer; CENTCOM denied any hit, and that remains an unverified competing claim. Two structural items matter more: the Wall Street Journal reported September 10 that advisers including Vance and Rubio have privately warned Trump the war could run through January 2029; and Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao acknowledged September 11 that Iran "blew the hell out of" NSA Bahrain, the Navy's main regional logistics hub, which will not resume service "anytime soon."

**The second chokepoint.** Between September 10 and 11, Houthi forces captured Mokha port, then Dhubab, Perim Island, and the Hanish Islands — positions overlooking the Bab al-Mandeb. That puts Iran-aligned forces beside the approaches to both of the Peninsula's oil chokepoints. A caveat worth stating: Houthi capability is not Iranian capability, and holding terrain near a strait isn't the same as closing it. But the direction is unmistakable.

**The economics keep tightening.** Brent settled at $104.61 and WTI at $100.05 on September 11 — the first close above $100 since mid-May, up over 8% on the week. US diesel topped $6 a gallon for the first time, and the IEA projected world oil supply falling 5.7 million barrels per day in 2026.

**Missile math.** The inventory figures are estimates and sources disagree. CSIS pegs remaining Patriots at 759-827, about a third of pre-war stock, while CNN's sources describe roughly half burned. On THAAD, CSIS estimates 234-278 remaining; CNN's sources say nearly 80% is gone. Both are open-source estimates of classified numbers, and Hegseth has disputed the depletion narrative. The FY27 munitions surge is real, but the Patriot and THAAD agreements are framework deals, not binding contracts, with deliveries at scale not expected until 2029-2030.

### Russian Drone Warfare — The Jet Generation Takes Altitude

Last week's baseline — roughly 3,000 jet-powered Geran-4/5 airframes a month — is now on the record: Ukraine's Defense Intelligence (DIU) told Militarnyi it produces about 3,000 monthly, with piston Geran-2s at roughly 2,800. That's an intelligence estimate, not confirmed trade data, though consistent with the observed launch tempo.

The tactical development matters more. DIU's updated specs put the Geran-4 cruising at 350-500 km/h with a 160 kgf turbojet and an 850 km range; an open-source tracker logged a single Geran-4 flight of 960 km, which would put 96% of Ukraine in range. DIU also reports Russia halted Geran-3 production in August to redirect engines to the more effective Geran-4/5 line.

The key adaptation: per Defense Express, jet Gerans now routinely fly above 5 kilometers and sometimes near 7, deliberately above the ceilings of Gepard, Stinger, Piorun and similar short-range systems — forcing medium- and long-range SAMs or F-16s to do the work, exactly the trade Russia wants. Launch figures reflect it: Ukraine's Air Force puts August jet-Geran launches between 2,500 and 2,800 depending on the source, up from roughly 1,500 in July. Overall August Shahed-type launches totaled about 5,155, roughly 81% intercepted per an open-source reconstruction of Air Force data. One caveat: Ukraine largely stopped publishing interception rates in August, so later figures are softer.

**Ground war.** ISW assesses Russia gained about 90 square kilometers in August, and only roughly 160 across the summer — against 1,440 in summer 2025, a roughly 90% decline. Ukrainian forces have largely eliminated the Russian salient north of Lyman. GUR head Ivashchenko, via The Times, assesses Russian generals have asked Putin for 800,000 more troops after the September 18-20 Duma elections — Ukrainian intelligence assessment, not independently verified. The Alabuga North Korean labor question remains unresolved: NHK reports up to 25,000 workers, HUR about 12,000, and no source has confirmed on-site headcount at scale.

### Ukrainian Drone Innovation — 3,000 Kilometers and an AI Threshold

On September 9, Ukrainian Special Operations Forces struck the Novy Urengoy gas-condensate plant in Yamalo-Nenets, Siberia — roughly 3,000 to 3,200 kilometers from Ukraine's border. The General Staff confirmed the strike and Russian officials confirmed a fire; the distance is the deepest of the war and the first strike into that Arctic region. Target identification comes from Ukrainian OSINT analysts, and the drone attribution to Fire Point's FP-1 is a manufacturer claim.

The pattern continued: the Saratov refinery struck September 7-8 and again September 11; the Novorossiysk naval base and fuel terminal September 8-9, where HUR claimed the frigate Admiral Essen was hit; the Slavyansk-on-Kuban refinery September 12-14. Independent CREA analysis found Russian fossil-fuel export revenue fell 8% month-on-month to about €604 million a day in August, with Novorossiysk crude loadings down 58% and halted nine consecutive days — the longest interruption since the invasion began.

**The autonomy threshold.** Brave1 reported September 8 that successful strikes using AI auto-homing increased tenfold since the start of 2026. In a first standardized trial, seven companies each flew a drone 2 km, acquired a moving light-armor target from beyond 500 meters, and struck autonomously with the pilot's hands off — six of seven passed and earned procurement recommendations.

**Interceptors.** SkyFall's P1-SUN, at a manufacturer-stated $1,000 a unit, now claims more than 5,500 Shaheds downed since November 2025, up from the 1,500 it claimed when I last wrote about it; its faster "JetKiller" variant entered mass production in August. Wild Hornets' Sting carries a manufacturer-listed cost between $2,100 and $2,500, with kill claims in the 7,000-9,000 range. All manufacturer claims. Ukraine's first codified jet interceptor, Alexa Spatium, entered service August 21 — the MoD released only its footprint, and a widely circulated "600 km/h" figure belongs to a different aircraft.

### Connecting the Threads

**The interceptor economy, from opposite directions.** The US is spending roughly 30 three-million-dollar Patriots to defend one Jordanian base against a single salvo. Ukraine is downing comparable drones with $1,000-2,500 airframes. Both wars turn partly on who spends less per engagement — and only one side has solved that problem.

**Russia's drone industry has a Chinese throat.** The Geran-4's performance leap rests on a higher-thrust Chinese turbojet; RBC-Ukraine, citing military sources, reports China has supplied only about 3,000 Telefly engines, roughly a sixth of assessed need. Russia is trying to localize engine production while Ukraine campaigns against the factories building the airframes.

**The clock is now political.** Trump tying the Iran war's end to November, and the Houthi seizures near Bab al-Mandeb, are linked: both speak to how long Washington can sustain this posture.

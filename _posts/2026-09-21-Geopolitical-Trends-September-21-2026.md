### Iran War — When the Official Numbers Become the Story

On September 18 the Washington Post reported at least 22 US troops killed — one official said 23 — against the Pentagon's acknowledged 18; Hegseth called it "fake." Treat the count as contested, the reporting as real. The Slotkin letter, signed by 47 senators, documents only eight press briefings since the war began, against more than seventy in Iraq's first month. When disclosure tightens this far, an absence of bad news stops being evidence of good news.

CBO puts the cost near $38 billion through August 1, rising about $3 billion a month; DoD says $42 billion. The House passed a war-powers resolution 220–204, its first success in three attempts, as analysts warn of a large funding gap against FY27 plans past the December 11 continuing resolution.

**Sustainment.** USS Abraham Lincoln left CENTCOM on August 22 after 274 days (USNI News), with a transit home likely pushing it past 300. USS Gerald R. Ford's 326 days was the longest since 1972, and USS George Washington's arrival as relief left the Western Pacific without a US carrier. On crew welfare, hard data is thin: family testimony through Navy Times and Military Times describes food shortages and two attempted overboard incidents, while the Navy says it has "not observed an increase" in suicidal ideation and declined to release figures. Admiral Cooper's claim of the lowest mental-health caseload of eleven carriers has no published data behind it; verifiable retention data is absent entirely.

**Attrition.** MQ-9 losses form a rising series — 11 early, 24 by April, at least 45 by August (about a quarter of the fleet), with the fleet falling from roughly 185 to 135 airframes; some were data-link failures, not shootdowns. Interceptor figures still disagree: CSIS estimates roughly 65% of Patriot inventory expended with under 1,000 remaining, THAAD down about 38% to 234–278; CNN's two anonymous sources say about half of Patriot and nearly 80% of THAAD. CSIS is the better-evidenced floor, CNN the pessimistic ceiling. Production binds either way — roughly 600–650 PAC-3 MSE a year, about 96 THAAD, replenishment into 2029–2030 — and Tomahawk runs the same way: over 1,000 fired, against production under 200 a year.

Iran's side is where estimates diverge most. Israeli assessments relayed by ISW in March put up to 80% of air defenses destroyed and 60–70% of launchers; US assessments reported by the Times and Post suggest Iran retained roughly 70% of prewar missiles but only about 40% of its drones. Those aren't reconcilable — the direction is depletion, the magnitude unknown. The economy is better evidenced: the rial near 2.2–2.25 million to the dollar, IMF inflation at 68.9%, oil exports around 210,000 barrels a day per Vortexa, with GDP projections running from −5.4% to −10.1% across July and September estimates.

**Hormuz.** War-risk insurance has gone from 0.10–0.25% of hull value before the war to 7.5–10% now, per Marsh and S&P Global — a $100 million tanker paying $3–10 million to transit, against roughly $250,000 before. Brent touched about $102 on September 17.

**This week.** On September 14, US forces destroyed two Iranian boats after the IRGC tried to seize a Navy drone in the strait. Houthi strikes pushed deeper into Saudi Arabia, reaching Riyadh on September 19 for the first time. On September 20 Iran warned of an imminent US attack; separately, Rezaei conveyed conditions through Qatari mediation — an end to the war on all fronts, release of frozen funds, lifting of the naval blockade.

### Russian Drone Warfare — Volume Down, Jet Share Up

The production baseline hasn't moved: roughly 3,000 Geran-4/5 airframes a month, per Ukraine's Defense Intelligence via Militarnyi. That's a single-source intelligence estimate, and it's the same figure I cited last week — a repeat, not new corroboration.

The mix did move. ISW's reconstruction has Shahed-type launches peaking at 8,150 in May and falling to 4,943 in August, while the jet-powered share of strike packages rose to roughly 56%. Last week's Air Force-based reconstruction put August near 5,155 — different methodology, so treat August as roughly 4,900–5,200. HUR puts the current Geran stockpile near 6,200.

The economics remain the story: a $2,500 Sting against a Geran-4 reported at $85,000–120,000 is roughly 40-to-1 in the defender's favor — but interception runs near 60% against jet-powered drones versus 90–95% against piston types, and Patriot scarcity binds on the fast movers.

Two calibration notes. North Korean labor at Alabuga stays unresolved — HUR says about 12,000, NHK and MSMT "as many as" 25,000, with no independent on-site confirmation of either. Banderol missile production is genuinely contested: DIU says about 80 a month, NordSINT near 250; I'm presenting both rather than picking. And an ArmyInform expert's argument that Russia has never launched even 100 jet Shaheds at once is worth holding alongside output figures — production and simultaneous-launch capacity are different constraints.

### Ukrainian Drone Innovation — The Largest Package of the War

On the night of September 19–20, Ukraine executed what ISW called the largest Ukrainian strike package since February 2022, timed to the final day of Duma elections. Targets included the Gazpromneft-Moscow refinery at Kapotnya; the General Staff said its AVT-6 primary unit was damaged, and Russian officials acknowledged strikes in 17 Moscow municipalities. Moscow's mayor claimed more than 1,600 drones intercepted — a belligerent claim, not independent. One identification remains unresolved: a system Zelensky called "Pelican," which a Russian milblogger says is the FP-7 ballistic missile and Ukrainian OSINT says is a one-way attack drone. The IEA estimates a Russian refinery was hit on average once every three days through the first eight months of 2026, with June throughput at 3.8 million barrels a day — the lowest in over twenty years, about 30% below a year earlier. Strike tallies differ by criteria (CNN's 305 since February 2022, the Baker Institute's 272 discrete events), so treat 272–305 as the range. Bloomberg, citing the Russian tax authority, has August oil and gas revenue down 16% year-on-year and oil revenue down more than 60% against July.

At sea, the September 9 Novorossiysk strike is now corroborated by satellite imagery showing damage to the frigates Admiral Essen and Admiral Makarov and a Buyan-M corvette — last week this rested on a single HUR claim. One thing not to conflate: there's no verified reporting that the Katran X1.2 sea drone has struck Russian naval or port assets; its documented use is trials and an undated "last autumn" attribution. Confirmed maritime strikes belong to Magura and Sea Baby USVs and Neptune missiles.

**Interceptors.** Ukraine's NSDC reports 100,000 produced in 2025 and a military claim that one in three Russian aerial targets is now downed by interceptors — state figures, not independently audited. Manufacturer claims deserve skepticism: SkyFall's P1-SUN tallies are internally inconsistent — "more than 1,500 Shaheds and 1,000 other drones in four months," "more than 3,000 since the start of 2026," and "more than 5,500 total." The 5,500 figure I repeated last week is one of three conflicting numbers from the same company, and I should have said so then. Wild Hornets claims more than 3,900 Geran kills as of March and output above 10,000 a month, unverified. Zelensky unveiled a new SSU interceptor drone shown downing a Geran-5 on September 19, and the private air-defense pool rose from 51 to 81 companies.

### Connecting the Threads

**Two interceptor economies, opposite outcomes.** The US is spending three-million-dollar Patriots against drones costing tens of thousands; Ukraine is spending $2,500 interceptors against $85,000–120,000 targets. Both wars turn on cost per engagement, and only one side has closed the gap.

**Fiscal attrition is the common denominator.** A $38 billion American bill rising $3 billion a month, against Russian oil and gas revenue down 16% year-on-year. Neither figure ends a war alone; both narrow the options.

**Disclosure is degrading in both theaters.** The Pentagon's briefing cadence has collapsed, and Ukraine stopped publishing missile-interception data in August. Fewer independent checks on belligerent claims.

Back in the r/CredibleDefense threads. Two things moved this week. In the Iran war, the Navy confirmed eight suicide attempts inside the USS Abraham Lincoln's strike group — which retires the "no increase in suicidal ideation" line the service gave reporters in August. And in Ukraine, the jet-drone interception rate is now being published as a number, 55%, which is both progress and a confession of how much of the sky is still open. Same rules as before: confirmed facts, intelligence estimates, and manufacturer claims kept apart.

### Iran War — The Disclosure Fight Becomes a Personnel Problem

The casualty accounting is no closer to resolution. The Pentagon's public database still shows 18 US dead; the Washington Post, citing six officials on September 18, reported at least 22. Secretary Hegseth called the reporting "fake." I'm carrying 18–23 as a range, not a fact. What did move is the wounded figure, which the Washington Post reported September 25 had risen to 861 after the Pentagon quietly added 29 sailors and 8 Marines, and a third September death classified "non-hostile."

But the week's real story is the Lincoln. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao wrote Senator Gillibrand on September 22 confirming **eight suicide attempts** across the carrier strike group — the carrier's crew, its air wing, a destroyer squadron staff and escorts — during a deployment that had passed 293 days. One sailor went overboard in early August and was recovered. This directly contradicts what the Navy told reporters in August: that it had "not observed an increase" in suicidal ideation. Admiral Brad Cooper's claim that Lincoln had the lowest mental-health caseload of the Navy's eleven carriers still has no published data behind it.

**Sustainment.** The rotation math is the same squeeze, wider. Ford came home in May after a record 326 days — that's history now, not a live marker, and I should have said so last week. Lincoln made its first port call of the deployment in Thailand in early September. George Washington arrived as relief, leaving the Western Pacific without a US carrier, and Theodore Roosevelt left San Diego on September 5 for a seven-month tour to backfill. Reporting suggests the Navy now plans two carrier strike groups in the Middle East through the end of 2026. On retention, the honest read is mixed: the Navy says enlisted retention is above goal in all three zones, while its own exit surveys put operational tempo at the top of why mid-career sailors leave. The logistics layer is worse: the hub moved from damaged Bahrain to Diego Garcia, two thousand miles away, forcing thirty-day advance food orders.

**Attrition.** MQ-9 losses hold at roughly 45, about a quarter of the prewar fleet, per the Washington Post's August reporting; the Air Force is accelerating a low-cost replacement. Interceptor depletion estimates still disagree: CSIS puts Patriot above 50% expended and THAAD at over half, while CNN's two anonymous sources say roughly half of Patriot but nearly 80% of THAAD. CBO's September 15 report says interceptors are as much as two-thirds consumed and prices munitions replacement at $21.7 billion. Tomahawk remains the clearest bind — more than 1,000 fired against a 2026 delivery count in the low hundreds.

**Iran's side.** The Statistical Center of Iran now reports GDP down 10.1% year-on-year in the spring quarter, with oil and gas down 26.4%. Twelve-month average inflation is running near 70%, the rial is above 2.2 million to the dollar, and Vortexa still puts crude exports at a floor of roughly 210,000 barrels a day. Gulf vulnerability cut both ways: a drone attack out of Iraq knocked out Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline at Yanbu, and Houthi missiles reached Riyadh again this month after a first strike on September 19.

**Diplomacy went backwards.** Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei had handed mediators a set of conditions around September 19–20 — end the war on all fronts, release frozen funds, lift the naval blockade. By September 25 that had hardened into a formal seven-day plan from Foreign Minister Araghchi: $12 billion in frozen assets released, sanctions lifted, blockade ended, all hostilities stopped — then Hormuz reopens and final-status talks begin on day seven. Trump rejected it, told aides per the Wall Street Journal that he expects to resume bombing after the November midterms, and on September 28 said the war would be won "very soon" without ruling out strikes before the vote. Araghchi said Iran is "fully prepared" for a "doomsday war." Trump calling talks "very good" while predicting renewed bombing is not a reconcilable position.

### Russian Drone Warfare — Flat Output, Rising Interception

The production baseline has not moved in six weeks: roughly 3,000 Geran-4/5 airframes a month, per Ukraine's Defense Intelligence via Militarnyi. That remains a single-source intelligence estimate, and repeating it is not corroboration. One genuine addition from HUR's Major General Skibitskyi: Russia is "completely dependent" on Chinese turbojets at $35,000–40,000 apiece — a production constraint dressed up as a production number.

Launch volume looks steady. ISIS's reconstruction puts August Shahed-type launches at roughly 4,800–5,300, with jet-powered types at 57–62% of the mix, consistent with last week's range. September is where the numbers get interesting: Zelensky said 2,730 jet drones were launched in the month with just over 1,500 downed, a 55% interception rate against a 90% target. The Air Force's Ihnat gave a slightly higher figure, about 60%, on a different counting window. I'd treat 55–60% as the honest range, and note that both figures are Ukrainian official sources. Reuters adds a caveat: a large interceptor trial in July largely failed.

North Korean labor is now better documented rather than resolved. The UN's monitoring team reported in September that 15,000–30,000 North Korean workers are in Russia, with up to 25,000 at the Alabuga plant — against HUR's older figure of about 12,000. Those aren't comparable numbers: one is a current presence, the other a 2025 plan. The trend to watch is ISW's assessment that Russia will sustain large-scale jet-drone strikes through the winter on the theory that penetration rates, not airframe counts, win this fight.

### Ukraine's Strike Campaign — Refineries, Not Headlines

The Moscow refinery at Kapotnya has stopped processing crude entirely. Both primary distillation units caught fire in the September 20 strike; Reuters reports repairs will take weeks and fuel is being pulled from the St. Petersburg exchange. Ukraine's General Staff claims more than 45% of Russia's design refining capacity is now disabled — a self-reported, unaudited state claim.

Then a cluster: the Ilsky refinery in Krasnodar was knocked out overnight September 25–26, with satellite imagery via Militarnyi indicating damage to three crude units, roughly 86% of designed capacity. Novoshakhtinsk was halted. Lukoil's Perm refinery — some 1,449 km from the border — suspended operations after September 25 strikes. On September 28 four oil depots in Krasnodar Krai were hit. Military-industrial targets came with them, including the Azov Optical-Mechanical Plant via Long Neptune. Some of those target-function attributions rest on Ukrainian claims with only partial open-source corroboration.

Still unresolved: the system Zelensky called "Pelican." Russian milbloggers say FP-7 ballistic missile; Ukrainian outlets say a one-way attack drone; ISW notes conflicting reporting. No progress on identifying it, so I'm leaving it open rather than picking a side. Same for the Katran X1.2: no verified strike on Russian naval or port assets. And the interceptor economics: unit costs span roughly $1,300–2,500, against Geran-4s at $85,000–120,000. Wild Hornets' new STING S is reported downing Geran-4s and Geran-5s, with mass production about a month out — manufacturer claims, unverified.

### Connecting the Threads

**Two interceptor economies, still opposite.** The US spends million-dollar interceptors against cheap drones and is well past halfway through its Patriot stock. Ukraine spends roughly $2,000 to kill an $85,000 target. Only one side has closed the cost gap, and it isn't the one with the larger budget.

**Personnel is the quiet constraint on both sides.** Eight suicide attempts in one carrier strike group is a sustainment datum, not a human-interest detail. Russia's version is Chinese engines and North Korean labor — dependency substituting for capacity.

**Negotiation and escalation are running at once.** Iran formalized terms; Washington rejected them and is signalling post-midterm escalation. That's not a dead channel, it's a channel both sides are using to buy time.

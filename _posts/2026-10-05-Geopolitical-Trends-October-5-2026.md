Back in the r/CredibleDefense threads. Three things moved this week, and one moves the wrong way. The USS Abraham Lincoln came home after 300-plus days at sea, and the Navy answered with a third carrier group, not a break. Iran's seven-day plan went nowhere and the rial hit a record low, yet Gulf crude exports climbed back above pre-war levels anyway. ### Iran War — The Carrier Comes Home, Replaced by Two More

The Lincoln is back — 302 days at sea as of September 19, per USNI News, the second-longest post-Cold War carrier deployment behind Gerald R. Ford's 326. Part of the strike group reached San Diego on October 2; local reporting puts the carrier itself home around October 8. But the Navy isn't reducing its presence. Theodore Roosevelt's strike group left San Diego September 27, the Makin Island amphibious group followed a day later, and the Wall Street Journal reported the moves could add 9,000 to 10,000 troops. By late November, three carriers and two amphibious groups are expected near Iran — what Al Jazeera, citing a US official, calls the largest regional concentration in decades. That's a buildup, not a relief.

The casualty ledger still doesn't reconcile: the Pentagon's public database sits near 19–20 dead, the Washington Post holds 22–23, and the wounded tally is 861 — contested ranges, not facts. New this week, an AP investigation (October 5) in which nine soldiers wounded in the March 1 Shuaiba strike say commanders underestimated the threat and the Army missed their brain injuries; the soldiers' accounts, not independently verified.

Two institutional signals: Hegseth ordered on September 30 a 20 percent cut to general and flag-officer billets by January 1, 2027 — roughly 170 of about 850, doubling an earlier target — and congressional Democrats have called for hearings on the eight confirmed Lincoln suicide attempts. Non-binding War Powers resolutions failed in both chambers.

**Attrition.** The MQ-9 fleet is down to about 135 aircraft with the production line closed, and at least 45 Reapers — roughly a quarter of the prewar inventory — were lost, per Washington Post reporting the service has never confirmed. Interceptor estimates still disagree: CSIS puts Patriot and THAAD each above 50 percent expended; CNN's two anonymous sources say roughly half of Patriot but nearly 80 percent of THAAD; CBO says up to two-thirds of the combined inventory. More than 1,000 Tomahawks have been fired against 2026 deliveries in the low hundreds.

On Hormuz, a real shift worth being precise about. Kpler data show Middle East crude exports above pre-war levels on four of seven days in late September — 19.5 to 22.5 million barrels a day against an 18 million pre-war average — as producers routed around the strait via pipelines and ship-to-ship transfers. The IRGC disputes it; commander Ali Fadavi says only three to four million barrels a day move and calls it negligible. Both can be partly true: total exports recovered while Hormuz transits stayed suppressed. A different metric from last week's transit count, not a contradiction. UKMTO still logs at least one attack a day since October 2. The G7 agreed on October 2 to release up to 100 million barrels from reserves; Brent eased to about $101 from $105, and Iran's rial broke 2.5 million to the dollar.

Diplomacy stalled. The seven-day roadmap was rejected; US ambassador Mike Waltz called frozen-funds and sanctions relief non-negotiable, and Araghchi said Iran is "fully prepared" to resume. Parliament speaker Ghalibaf said October 4 that Hormuz stays closed until Washington accepts the plan. Regionally, the Saudi-led coalition blamed a September 29 drone strike on Medina's Taibah power station on the Houthis, who deny it.

### Russian Drone Warfare — The Engine Story Breaks

The week's most consequential item is an intelligence disclosure. Ukraine's Defense Intelligence (DIU), via its War&Sanctions portal, reported October 5 that the upgraded Geran-4 now flies with a locally produced Alabuga turbojet, replacing the Chinese Telefly TF-TJ2000A — plus a US-sourced magnetometer module, a "Susanin" electro-optical navigation system, and a KRSN shaped-charge warhead built to sever bridges and power pylons. That's one agency's estimate, not independently verified. It nonetheless qualifies the line I carried last week — HUR's Skibitskyi calling Russia "completely dependent" on Chinese turbojets. Kyiv Independent still cites Chinese engines for the Geran-5, so the two aren't strictly contradictory, but this is the first genuine challenge to the dependency story. A separate new Geran-5 variant ("K5") with autonomous optical navigation was reported September 27, from a single monitoring channel and only partially corroborated since.

Launch volume set a record: Air Force official Ihnat said Russia launched 3,177 jet-powered drones by September 29, exceeding all of August's 2,850. Zelensky's September 26 window showed more than 2,730 launched and more than 1,500 downed — 55 percent — and his October 4 figures were 85–92 percent for conventional Shaheds but only 50–60 percent for jet-powered ones. So 50–60 remains the honest range against a 90 percent target. One 12-hour window hit 84 percent; that's an outlier, not the average.

Production: HUR's ~3,000 a month is now two months stale and single-sourced; the Come Back Alive foundation, via CBS, estimates about 2,000 for September, so the defensible range is 2,000–3,000. No new North Korean figure emerged — the monitoring team's up-to-25,000 at Alabuga against HUR's older ~12,000 still measures two different things. ISW added an October 5 report on Russian "quasi-cruise missiles" (the Banderol and the new Dan-T) as a new low-cost tier.

### Ukraine's Strike Campaign — Refineries and, Now, Pipelines

Ukraine's Defense Ministry claimed October 4 that long-range strikes have "put out of action" more than 51 percent of Russia's oil refining capacity — self-reported, unaudited, no published methodology, and AP said explicitly it couldn't verify it. The metric also shifted from last week's ">45 percent of design capacity," so it's a claimed escalation, not a confirmed one. The one partial independent acknowledgment: Putin said October 1 the strikes cost roughly 1 percent of GDP.

The campaign widened. Overnight October 2, drones hit Transneft's Samara tank farm — described as Europe's largest — and the Volgograd refinery area, confirmed by multiple outlets and eyewitness video. SBU drones reportedly struck Su-35 and Su-34 fighters and an ammunition depot at Khanskaya airfield in Adygea, about 500 km deep; a fire was geolocated, but Russia hasn't confirmed losses.

The "Pelican" puzzle is half-solved: Ukraine's FP-7 ballistic missile saw first combat use October 1, per Zelensky, though its roughly 250 km range limits it to border regions, and Guardian-reported sources still describe Pelican as a separate long-range drone. Five interceptor models are in testing, with about 400 units of one due by end-October, and AI-aimed gun turrets — 12 around Kyiv — claimed their first jet-drone kills, including a Geran-5, per Ihnat. Germany's Merz arrived in Kyiv on October 4 with roughly $1.1–1.46 billion in state aid plus company deals, including funding for tens of thousands of Quantum Systems interceptors built in Ukraine — a manufacturer figure with no schedule. And still no verified Katran X1.2 strike; the Black Sea story this week runs the other way, with Russian drones sinking a Turkish-owned grain ship near Romania, killing two.

### Connecting the Threads

**Two interceptor economies, still opposite.** The US spends million-dollar interceptors on cheap drones and is well past halfway through its Patriot stock; Ukraine spends about $2,000 to kill an $85,000 airframe.

**Personnel and industrial base are one constraint in two coats.** Three carriers and 20 percent fewer admirals is not a service at rest, and Russia's answer to sanctions is import substitution while the US closes its Reaper line. Both wars are supply-chain wars now: the headlines are strikes, the trend line is who can keep the line running.

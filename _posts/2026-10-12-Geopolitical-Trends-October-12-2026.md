### Iran War — The Export Rebound Reverses

Last week I flagged Kpler data showing Middle East crude exports above pre-war levels on four of seven late-September days. That picture has flipped. Hormuz exports fell to roughly 8.5 million barrels a day for the week ending October 8 — about 40 percent below normal — and the Middle East total to 15.3 million barrels a day, about 10 percent below pre-war, per Kpler via CNBC. Windward shows 31 ships a day transiting in late September against 138 pre-war; both are open-source estimates. IRGC commander Fadavi disputes all Western figures, saying only 3–4 million barrels a day move; CENTCOM says 20 million barrels transited and rejects the "closed" framing. Both can be partly true, but the direction this week is contraction, not recovery.

The week ending October 4 saw 11 tankers attacked, the most since the war began, per IMO data via CNBC, and 15–16 ships have been targeted in October so far. At least 24 sailors have died across 100 commercial ships since February.

New this week, the Houthi-Saudi front bled directly into the oil complex. Riyadh's King Khalid airport was struck twice in two days, October 10–11, the deadliest attack in any Gulf state since the war started. And Saudi officials now tell the Wall Street Journal they are low on interceptors — shifting batteries to protect palaces and energy infrastructure and asking France, Britain, Pakistan and Egypt for help. That is a downstream effect of the same US stockpile depletion I have tracked for two months, and the first time the shortage has visibly constrained a partner rather than the US itself.

The morale ledger moved, quietly. The Pentagon raised its official toll to 21 killed and 865 wounded on October 9 — still below the Washington Post's internal figure of 23, and the two added deaths were non-combat. USS Abraham Lincoln came home October 8 after roughly 321–322 days at sea, a genuine source disagreement: the Navy's own release says 321, Al Jazeera and AP say 322. Consecutive days at sea diverge too, 265 versus 286 depending on whether you count the Thailand port call. On any reading, it is the second-longest carrier deployment since the Cold War. Hegseth greeted the crew and awarded the strike group a Presidential Unit Citation; the eight confirmed suicide attempts remain under congressional scrutiny. Diplomacy is frozen on purpose: Trump said October 8 that talks are "productive" but the blockade stays in force, and reporting indicates no strikes before the November 3 midterms — while the Pentagon prepares post-midterm options.

### Russian Drone Warfare — September's Real Number

The September launch figure firmed up. Air Force spokesman Yuriy Ihnat now puts full-September jet-powered launches at about 3,500, against 3,177 as of September 29 and 2,850 for all of August — a real escalation, though it remains a Ukrainian official count, not an audited one. The October rate has visibly eased to roughly 80 jet-powered a day, 50 at night and 30 by day. Interception is the contested part: Zelensky put jet-powered interception at 57 percent on September 28, while official daily rates in early October ran 72–78 percent on several nights, and one expert called an 82.5 percent night a record outlier. The honest range is still 50–82 percent depending on the night, well short of the better-than-90 percent Ukraine achieves against propeller Shaheds.

On propulsion, the DIU's October 5 disclosure — a US-sourced magneto-inductive sensor, a new "Susanin" optical navigation system, a KRSN shaped-charge warhead, and a Russian-built Alabuga turbojet replacing the Chinese Telefly — is a single-agency intelligence estimate, re-reported widely but not independently verified. The important qualification: the newer Geran-5 and the Banderol S8000 still fly Chinese engines. So the "China is the bottleneck" line holds for the higher-value airframes even as the older Geran-4 localises. Unit cost for a Geran-5 is estimated near $115,000, with the engine alone about $40,000.

Two tactical notes. ISW's October 7 assessment argues the jet-powered Gerans let Russia concentrate cruise and ballistic missiles on a smaller target set without raising missile expenditure — fewer targets, more waves. EW hardening continues too: drones hitting Kyiv now carry 32-element controlled reception pattern antennas, up from 16. The October 6–7 package was the template: 48 cruise missiles, ballistic and anti-ship rounds, and 130 strike and decoy drones, 161 of them downed per Ukraine's Air Force. On North Korean labour, the 11-country monitoring team's figure of up to 25,000 at Alabuga sits awkwardly beside the same report's roughly 1,000 at "strategically important facilities," while HUR's older ~12,000 measures something else again. No single verified number resolves it.

### Ukraine's Strike Campaign — Omsk and the Data Centers

Ukraine's Defense Ministry says long-range strikes have put 51 percent of Russia's refining capacity "out of action." That remains self-reported, unaudited, and AP says it cannot verify it. Independent commercial data cuts the other way: Kpler assesses Russian refineries operating at roughly 60 percent of installed capacity. Read the 51 percent as a claimed ceiling, not a measured outage.

The strikes themselves are better documented. On October 8, Ukrainian FP-1 drones flew more than 2,500 km to hit the Omsk refinery — Russia's largest, around 22 million tonnes a year — confirmed by Ukraine's General Staff and multiple outlets. Satellite analysis puts the earlier Volgograd strike at roughly 70 percent of primary capacity knocked out, and Reuters confirmed the halt. The clearest multi-outlet-verified new category is Russian AI infrastructure: Yandex data centers were hit at Sasovo/Ryazan, Kaluga and Vladimir across October 8–11, damage Yandex itself confirmed.

On the water, two milestones. Ukraine's Navy says a Sargan-3000 uncrewed surface vessel used a 12.7 mm Kongsberg Protector mount to shoot down a Russian Shahed on October 7 — an official claim, reported by multiple outlets, and the first aerial kill by a USV using a gun rather than a missile. And on October 6 Ukrainian naval drones set the Liberian-flagged Aframax Rio ablaze off Sochi, all 23 crew rescued, per RFE/RL and Meduza. Still no verified Katran X1.2 strike; that remains a manifested capability.

Counter-drone. AI-aimed gun turrets scored their first jet-powered kills, including a Geran-5, per Ihnat — 12 installed, eight more planned for Kyiv Oblast. Interceptor production is real but engine-limited, and the official numbers disagree badly: Zelensky puts the Bars interceptor at roughly 30 percent, while Defense Minister Khmara claimed five-for-five, 100 percent, on October 11. Germany's October 4 package is still a range — €1.1 billion per Breaking Defense versus €1.3 billion, or $1.46 billion, per Zelensky — and the "tens of thousands" of Quantum Systems interceptors remains a manufacturer figure with no confirmed delivery schedule.

### Connecting the Threads

Two interceptor economies, now visibly colliding. The same Patriot and THAAD depletion the US has run down in the Iran theater is what has left Saudi Arabia short and defending palaces instead of cities. The shortage has stopped being an American internal problem and become a coalition one.

And the industrial-base logic is uniform. Ukraine hits the refinery and the data center; Russia answers with volume at the launcher and the interceptor line; Washington closes its Reaper line while ramping Patriot production on multi-year timelines. The headlines are strikes. The trend line is still who can keep the line running.

Back in the r/CredibleDefense threads. The story this week is timing, not a new weapon. Washington has frozen its Iran strikes until the November 3 midterms; Kyiv, now reportedly out of Patriot interceptors, hit Russian oil and digital infrastructure harder than at any point since 2022; and the Army chief the Pentagon ousted in April had warned, before the war, that the interceptor shortage would last until 2030. Same rules as always: confirmed facts, intelligence estimates, and manufacturer claims kept apart.

### Iran War — The Blockade, and the Bill Coming Due

UKMTO's week-to-October-16 overview puts Hormuz transits roughly 70 percent below pre-conflict, with 12 attacks and 84 incidents since February — likely an undercount, since UKMTO concedes most attacked vessels never report. The New York Times counts 26 vessels struck in October as of the 15th, after 11 attacks in the week to October 4, the wartime high; IMO data puts at least 24 sailors dead across roughly 100 commercial ships. On October 18 the IRGC said two tankers, about two million barrels each, caught fire after hitting naval mines — a single-source, unnamed claim, not an event.

The flow data still disagrees with itself. Kpler shows the week to October 8 at roughly 8.5 million barrels a day, about 40 percent below normal, with a 20.8-million-barrel day on October 8 collapsing to 2.9 million on October 11; TankerTrackers counters that more than 12 million a day have moved this month. Iran's own exports are unambiguous — zero seaborne crude through early October, per Vortexa. Brent sat near $108 on Monday.

The week's real disclosure is a warning that arrived too late. CBS reported that Gen. Randy George, then Army chief of staff, flagged in late December — in the Pentagon's fiscal 2027 force-allocation plan, two months before the war — that THAAD and Patriot stocks would stay "strategically low" until 2030. Hegseth removed him in April. The arithmetic agrees: CSIS estimates the US began 2026 with about 2,300 Patriot interceptors and fired 1,050–1,430, and 355 THAAD rounds of which 190–290 went. CBO's September baseline covers the rest — one-half to two-thirds of the combined Patriot, THAAD, SM-3 and SM-6 inventory consumed, a five-year-plus rebuild. Costs run from $38.1 billion through August 1 to $43.6 billion as of September 3.

Posture reflects the pause, not the shortage. USNI's October 13 tracker shows two carrier strike groups on station in the Arabian Sea, with USS Theodore Roosevelt en route — potentially a third by late October. USS Abraham Lincoln got home October 8 after roughly 321 days, the second-longest carrier deployment since the Cold War, credited with 3,693 sorties and 450 Tomahawks. Its eight confirmed suicide attempts remain under congressional scrutiny; RUSI notes eight of the last 11 carrier deployments ran eight months or longer. The shortage is visible abroad too — Saudi Arabia is low enough on interceptors to be guarding palaces over cities.

### Iran — Money, Missiles, and a New Defense Minister

The squeeze shows in what Iran cannot hide. The rial is down about 35 percent against the dollar since July and annual inflation hit 73.6 percent in September, per Iranian official data via Reuters. CENTCOM says the blockade has denied Iran at least $25 billion in oil exports; the "IRGC can't pay its proxies" element is a US assertion, not verified. Missiles are the part the bombing never fixed — US intelligence assessed in May that Tehran retained about 70 percent of its prewar missile stockpile and launchers. JINSA counts at least 595 Iranian launches since July 7. Parliament confirmed Mehrdad Akhlaghi as defense minister on October 18, with Lavrov in Tehran the next day.

Diplomacy is frozen on purpose and pointed past election day. Trump has said no strikes before November 3; the Wall Street Journal reports he rejected Iran's seven-day ceasefire — which would have reopened Hormuz for a lifted blockade — and expects to resume bombing afterward. Foreign Minister Araghchi says the six-day offer stands. CNN reports US options could include the GBU-43/B MOAB.

### Russian Drone Warfare — September's Bill and Millerovo's Pads

September's launch figure firmed up, and it is large. Air Force spokesman Yuriy Ihnat told the Wall Street Journal Russia fired 3,520 jet-powered Gerans in September and intercepted only 54 percent, alongside roughly 4,900 piston-engine drones — a Ukrainian official count, not audited. ISIS reconstructs about 6,700 Shahed-type launches including decoys for the month, roughly 7,400–7,700 full-month, up about 30 percent on August.

Two industrial facts matter more than any strike. Russia built a new Geran-5 launch site near Millerovo in about six weeks — eight launch positions and 32 preparation pads, with satellite imagery showing drones arriving October 12, per Reuters; DroneSec assesses it could fire 100-plus jet drones a day. And a recovered Geran-4 flight controller catalogued 105 components, at least 80 (roughly 76 percent) from US manufacturers. Ukraine also captured a 32-element controlled reception pattern antenna, up from 16, raising jamming resistance. North Korean manpower stays unresolved — HUR's up-to-12,000 at Alabuga against the UN monitoring team's 15,000–30,000 in Russia — so I report the range.

The weekly packages were relentless: the Air Force via ISW logged 39 missiles plus 128 drones (77 jet) on October 13–14; 580 drones on October 14–15; 332 drones plus Iskanders, KN-23s and Kalibrs on October 16–17; and 16 ballistic missiles plus 269 drones on October 18–19. Russia ran 87 targeted attacks on Ukraine's energy sector in the week to October 19; the last wave hit 28 substations and left at least 550,000 households without power. Zelensky put the week at almost 2,700 attack drones, 1,700 bombs and 66 missiles, most ballistic.

Interception against jet-powered drones is the contested number: 54 percent for September per Ihnat, about 55–60 percent in early October per Zelensky, 76 percent by mid-October again per Ihnat, and an 82.5 percent single night on October 7 that one expert flagged as an outlier. Against propeller Shaheds Ukraine still runs better than 90 percent — which is why ISW expects Russia to pair ballistic missiles with jet drones through the winter.

### Ukraine's Strikes — St. Petersburg and the Refineries

Ukraine's long-range campaign escalated twice. Overnight October 17–18, FP-1 drones struck St. Petersburg in what ISW calls Ukraine's largest attack on Leningrad Oblast since 2022: the Petersburg Oil Terminal, the Ruchyi fuel depot, a VK data center and adjacent substation, and a 79,000-square-metre Ozon logistics hub. Fires broke out in four districts and about 20,000 people lost power; the governor put the toll at one killed and five injured. Russia's Defense Ministry claims it downed 1,015 drones overnight — unverified — with the Leningrad governor claiming 145 in his region alone.

Four days earlier, on October 15, Ukrainian drones suspended one of the Ryazan refinery's crude units, resuming the refinery campaign after Zelensky said he was "done" with the US-brokered energy truce. On October 14–15 a Navy Long Neptune strike hit Borisoglebsk airfield: the Navy claims six Su-34Ms destroyed, satellite imagery read by AviVector shows four destroyed and two badly damaged, and Russia acknowledged nothing. On the refining arithmetic, Ukraine's Defense Ministry repeats "more than 51 percent" of capacity out of action with no stated denominator; Kpler counts roughly 1.5–2.0 million barrels a day genuinely offline, nearer a quarter of the IEA's baseline. Read 51 percent as a claimed ceiling.

The real constraint on Ukraine's defense showed up this week. CNN reported, from a single source, that Ukraine has run out of Patriot interceptors entirely, leaving almost all the ballistic missiles in the last strike unintercepted. On interceptor drones the official numbers still contradict each other — Zelensky puts Bars at about 30 percent of jet-drone kills while Defense Minister Khmara claimed a five-for-five, 100 percent result from one unit, a different metric — and Wild Hornets' 80–90 percent hit rate is a manufacturer claim. At sea, a Sargan-3000 USV downed a Shahed with a Kongsberg machine-gun mount on October 7, a first.

### Connecting the Threads

One shortage now shapes three theaters. The same Patriot and THAAD depletion that froze American options in the Gulf emptied Ukraine's launchers and left Saudi Arabia guarding palaces instead of cities. Randy George saw the hole before the war and was removed for saying so.

And the industrial-base logic is uniform. Russia stands up a launch pad in six weeks and installs American chips in its drones; Ukraine strikes the refinery and the data center; Washington rebuilds interceptors on multi-year contracts and lets a carrier come home after 321 days. The headlines are strikes. The trend line is still who can keep the line running.

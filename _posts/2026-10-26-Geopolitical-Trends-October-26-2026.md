### Iran — The Blockade's Bill, and a Disputed Missile Number

Brent settled at $116.72 on October 23, its highest close since April 29 and up about 6 percent on the week, and the IEA's Fatih Birol said Monday there is no credible pathway back to pre-war energy conditions in 2027. October is the war's worst month for shipping, though the count depends on who counts: the IMO has 27 attacks on commercial ships against 22 in March, the Washington Institute 28 in October, and UKMTO 135 incidents by October 16 — an undercount by its own admission. Transits are the cleaner measure and are still wrecked: 254 in the week to October 14, about 70 percent below pre-conflict. Two ships sank in October, and CENTCOM's Adm. Brad Cooper says more than 20 mariners have been killed since February. Cooper's $25 billion denial-of-revenue figure is a single agency's declaration; the commercial data agrees anyway. Kpler records zero Iranian crude loadings in September, the first zero month since 1979, and Vortexa has October crude at zero with LPG down 48 percent month-on-month.

The disputed number is the arsenal. US intelligence assessed in May that Iran retained about 70 percent of its prewar missile stockpile and launchers. This brief carried that last week; this week Cooper publicly rejected it. Treat it as contested, not settled. JINSA counts at least 604 launches since July 7; inflation is 73.6 percent, and the IMF projects a 7.6 percent contraction. Diplomacy is frozen by choice: Trump said October 25 there would be no strike that day, while CNN reported US forces could launch major operations within 96 hours of an order. Iran's seven-day Hormuz-for-blockade offer stands unanswered. The Journal, aboard USS George Washington on October 24, watched a heavy rearming of land-attack and bunker-buster munitions. The pause is political; the loadout is not.

### Iran — The Home Front, the Fleet, and the Floor Under the Casualty Count

The carrier picture did not develop as expected: USNI's October 19 tracker shows two strike groups in the Arabian Sea — George Washington and George H.W. Bush — with Theodore Roosevelt still in Japan, the projected third carrier not yet arrived. The strain is structural: Hegseth has extended Middle East deployments into 2027, and USNI records maintenance availabilities pushed from 2026 into 2027.

USS Abraham Lincoln got home October 8 after 322 days, including 265 consecutive at sea, with eight suicide attempts across the strike group confirmed by the Navy secretary's letter. CNN quoted crew describing "very low" morale, but also reported that a carrier deployment typically sees one or two attempts a year, and that the strike group's rate was roughly in line with the Navy-wide 2024 figure — the deployment was unprecedented; the figure was not. The official toll of 21 killed and 865 wounded is likewise a floor, quietly raised from 18 killed in October.

Iranian pressure is economic, not military: mobile tariffs rose up to 40 percent on October 23, and Amnesty documented five protest-related executions between September 30 and October 5. It and Human Rights Watch both flag IRGC recruitment of children as young as 12; the government's claimed 33 million "self-sacrifice" sign-ups come with no evidence. Desertion claims remain a single opposition outlet's March snapshot, with no October corroboration.

### Russian Drone Warfare — Lower Volume, New Variants, and a Speed Cap

The week's launch volume went down, not up. Zelensky's tally for the week to October 25 was about 1,600 guided bombs, 48 missiles and roughly 2,300 attack drones — against 2,700 drones, 1,700 bombs and 66 missiles the week before. Drones down about 15 percent, missiles about 27 percent, bombs flat — a Ukrainian official count, not an audited one.

The week's real story was a dependency. From October 14 to 24 SpaceX capped in-motion speed on Ukraine's Starshield package at roughly 160 km/h, down from about 470. Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces said the cap degraded intermediate-range strike and counter-drone work; ISW assessed the jet-drone interception rate fell from 77 percent to about 74 percent, its own framing being "slightly lower but still relatively high." Service was restored October 24. Attribution is unresolved: the Journal blames Musk, Reuters cites US officials saying the order came from inside the White House, and Musk says Starshield is controlled by the US military. Three sources, three answers.

Two new Geran-5 derivatives surfaced — one with the engine inside the fuselage and optical navigation that largely defeats jamming, one seeking 900 km/h, an aspiration with "no actual measurement data available yet," per Ukrainian officials. Production figures still disagree, and HUR raised its own: about 4,000 jet-powered Geran-4/5 a month against DIU's August figure of 3,000, which RUSI still cites. One correction to last week's brief: the 15,000-30,000 North Korean figure is the UN monitoring team's Russia-wide total, not Alabuga's, where the CSIS-Bush Institute count is 12,000.

A Kyiv Independent investigation adds the month's best tactical note: Gerans now carry Chinese mesh radios that let one drone relay another's signal — during the September 29 Inkom strike in Kyiv, a jet Shahed circled at about 3 km relaying while the strike drone went in. The 63-hop figure is the outlet's, unverified.

### Ukraine's Strikes — Five Plants in 48 Hours

The deep-strike campaign moved from refineries to components. Between October 22 and 25 the General Staff confirmed hits on the Proton-Electrotex semiconductor plant at Oryol — ISW-reviewed satellite imagery shows two buildings damaged, and the regional governor reported three killed — the Tolyattikauchuk and Omsky Kauchuk chemical plants, and the Energia plant at Yelets, which the General Staff says is Russia's sole maker of batteries for UMPB D-30SN glide-bomb guidance modules. Downstream, Kpler data via CNBC put September diesel exports at 141,000 barrels a day, down more than 75 percent year-on-year.

Russia's answer is manufacturing plus retail terror: four Epicentr DIY hypermarkets hit in under 24 hours on October 24-25, with a follow-on strike in Kharkiv aimed at the arriving rescuers.

### The Interceptor Economy

Both sides now build cheap interceptors. Russia's Sokol-I, a polystyrene hand-launched design, is per HUR in mass production at a stated capacity of up to 60,000 a month at roughly $4,000 each — a single unverified intelligence estimate. Ukraine's F-Drones F-Jet 80 targets $5,000-7,000 a unit; ISW frames the contrast as $7,000 for an interceptor drone against roughly $1 million for an F-16's air-to-air missile. Helsing unveiled two Contra interceptors on October 26 at under $100,000 a shot, market-ready only in late 2027 — manufacturer claims. Scale has not moved: Ukraine's deputy air force commander says 14 companies are testing jet interceptors, none delivering at scale before late November.

Patriot resupply is where the numbers openly conflict. CNN's single-source report that Ukraine had run out entirely was corroborated by the Washington Post, which also cites Ukrainian officials and a NATO representative. Zelensky says US deliveries fell to about 12 a month against a need of roughly 350 — a US official denies it — with planned deliveries falling from 675 in 2023 to 264 for 2026. The clearest new capability was seaborne: Ukraine's Navy says a Sargan 3000 USV launched a Sting interceptor drone that downed a Russian Shahed over the Black Sea.

### Connecting the Threads

Black Sea spillover is no longer incidental. A Comoros-flagged cargo ship sank about 24 km off Constanța on October 23, killing one sailor and leaving three missing; Romania blamed Russia and its jets downed two Russian drones over Romanian territorial waters on October 24, while NATO is sending patrol aircraft, radars and drones. The alliance still will not publicly attribute the shipping attacks to Russia — the part to watch, because seven cargo ships and roughly 30 sailors killed or missing in two weeks is a pattern looking for a policy.

Strip the headlines back and one line covers both theaters: Iran's oil revenue is heading to zero and its arsenal number is disputed; Russia's drone volume fell while its designs advanced; Ukraine's industrial campaign worked, and its air defence was constrained for ten days by a speed cap on an American satellite service. The strikes set the news cycle; the trend line is who can keep the line running.

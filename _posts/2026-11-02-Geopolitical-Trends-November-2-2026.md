Back in the r/CredibleDefense threads. Two things moved this week that the strike reports miss. The war got its first successful limpet-mine attack — fast-attack craft placed two charges on a tanker's hull off Khasab — and Brent fell more than 6 percent on diplomacy the negotiators themselves do not believe in. Iran's delegation left Doha saying the ball is in Washington's court, a draft proposal is circulating, and Iranian officials briefed that they now consider a return to war likelier than a deal. ### Iran — The Mines, the Money, and a Diplomatic Window Nobody Believes

The Louise 1, Panama-flagged, took two limpet mines about 25 nautical miles northeast of Khasab, Oman, on October 29 (some outlets date it October 30); the engine room caught fire and her 27 Indian crew were unhurt. UKMTO verified the report and ISW assessed it as the war's first successful limpet-mine attack — after CENTCOM announced mine-clearing in late August, Iran has an interest in making the strait look mined again. Tehran's claim of eleven tankers ablaze since October 25 is single-origin and stays out of the accounting.

October was the worst month for shipping since March: UKMTO logged 135 incidents by October 16, the IMO 120 since the war began; definitions differ enough that the honest statement is "roughly 27 to 35 attacks in October." Transits are the cleaner measure and run about 70 percent below pre-war levels; IMF PortWatch recorded two on October 25 against a pre-crisis norm near 85 a day. War-risk insurance is now 3 to 10 percent of hull value against 0.15 to 0.25 percent before the war.

Oil, meanwhile, fell. Brent closed October 30 at $113.43 and dropped more than 6 percent on November 2 — reports ranged from about $105 to $112 depending on when in the session they were taken — on the belief that talks might produce a reopening. Iran says it can reopen Hormuz in two days if the US lifts the blockade, removes recent sanctions, releases frozen assets and draws down troops. Vance wrote to Iran's security council secretary; Netanyahu is pressing Trump to strike after the midterms; Rubio says no decision has been made.

### Iran — The Fleet, the Ledger, and the Bill

Sustainment is where the cost sits. MCPON Perryman and CNO Caudle said on October 27–28 that 10-to-11-month deployments will be the Navy's standard for "a couple years" — the rotational fix is now policy, not exception. The Pentagon's official toll moved from 21 to 23 killed and 865 wounded on October 29; the wounded figure has consistently been a floor. War cost reached $47.1 billion through October 16, with munitions above 60 percent of it. At least 45 MQ-9 Reapers, roughly a quarter of the fleet, are gone for more than $1.3 billion. On interceptors two credible estimates agree in scale: CSIS puts Patriot expenditure at about 1,060–1,430 rounds, roughly 65 percent of the pre-war inventory, while CBO told Congress the war consumed half to two-thirds of the combined Patriot, THAAD, SM-3 and SM-6 stocks, with rebuilding taking at least five years. A third carrier and 9,000–10,000 more troops are due by the end of November.

Three corrections. USS Abraham Lincoln's deployment was 321 days per USNI News, not the 322 this brief carried. The dispute over Iran's missile stockpile is documented as CENTCOM chief Adm. Brad Cooper's testimony of May 14, not an October event; the October dating this brief used does not hold up. And the IMF projects Iranian inflation at about 78.6 percent for 2026 — the 73.6 percent carried here could not be sourced.

### Russian Drone Warfare — Production Up, Volume Down, and a Capability Ladder

October's totals, from the Ukrainian Air Force: more than 4,000 jet-powered drones, about 4,740 conventional Shahed-type drones, roughly 360 Banderol drone-launched cruise missiles, some 150 ballistic and 80 cruise or air-launched missiles. The week to October 25, by Zelensky's tally, was about 2,300 attack drones against 2,700 the week before — volume down, capability up.

Production remains a range, not a number. Ukrainian intelligence put jet-powered Geran-4/5 output near 3,000 a month in August; HUR raised that to roughly 4,000 in late October; RUSI still cites 3,000; a presidential adviser gives 3,000 jet plus 700 Geran-5. CSIS's figure above 5,500 a month is Alabuga's all-type capacity, not jet output. Two new Geran-5 derivatives surfaced: one with the engine buried inside the fuselage behind a top intake and optical navigation that Ukrainian officials say largely defeats jamming, another box-shaped with a V-tail. The 900 km/h target is an aspiration — officials concede there is no measurement data.

The more useful development is the navigation stack. DIU's October assessment of the Geran-4 describes a magnetometer module built on a US-made sensor, an inertial unit to cut gyro drift, electro-optical terrain matching, and a new shaped-charge warhead meant to cut bridge spans and power-line supports. Mesh radios are confirmed inside the airframes; during the September 29 strike on a Kyiv business centre, a jet Shahed circled at about 3 km relaying for the strike drone. The widely repeated "63 hops" is the radio's device ceiling, not a combat observation. Interception of the jet types sits in a 60-to-80-percent band; ISW's dip from 77 to about 74 percent during the Starshield speed cap is the cleanest like-for-like figure. Service was restored October 24.

### Ukraine's Strikes — Components, Not Refineries (Mostly)

The October campaign kept hitting the parts chain: Proton-Electrotex at Oryol (October 23, microchips), Tolyattikauchuk (October 24), Omsky Kauchuk in Siberia (October 24; satellite imagery shows damaged process units), and the Energia plant at Yelets (October 25–26), which the General Staff says makes batteries for aerial-bomb planning-and-correction modules — the "UMPB D-30SN" designation circulating elsewhere does not appear in the sourcing. Novokuibyshevsk refinery was hit October 30, with geolocated footage. A reported strike on the NORSI refinery at Kstovo on November 1 remains unconfirmed; Russian authorities acknowledged damage to an industrial site in the region.

Diesel needs calibrating too. Kpler's 141,000 barrels a day in September, down more than 75 percent year-on-year, is a September snapshot. With Moscow lifting its export ban on October 9, October exports ran more than three times September's level.

### The Interceptor Economy — Cheap Drones, Expensive Politics

Both sides build cheap interceptors. Russia's Sokol-I, per HUR a polystyrene hand-launched design at a claimed capacity of 60,000 a month and about $4,000 each, remains a single-source intelligence estimate. Ukraine's program is scaling slower than its ambition: fourteen companies are flight-testing jet interceptors, six with kills, and officials do not expect delivery at scale before late November. F-Drones' F-Jet 80 carries a $5,000–7,000 target price — a manufacturer claim without independent combat confirmation. Germany's Helsing unveiled two Contra interceptors on October 26 at under $100,000 a shot, market-ready in late 2027. The published Ukrainian program cap is $20,000–25,000 per launch against an estimated $100,000–150,000 for a jet-powered Shahed.

At sea, Ukraine's Navy says a Sargan 3000 USV launched a Sting interceptor that downed a Russian Shahed over the Black Sea — a service claim with video but no independent confirmation. Katran X1.2 carries up to 27 AI-guided interceptors.

Air defence is the unresolved conflict. Zelensky says US Patriot deliveries average about 12 a month against a need of roughly 350; NATO's supreme commander says everything ordered has been delivered and nothing delayed. Both are on the record; neither is confirmed. The State Department cleared a $2.1 billion sale of 500 PAC-3 MSE interceptors on October 30 — paid for by Canada, Germany, Norway and an EU loan — but the missiles are not expected until the 2030s.

### Connecting the Threads

Strip the headlines back: Russia's drone volume fell while its navigation and warhead designs advanced; Ukraine's component campaign bit, and the diesel damage partly healed; Iran's oil revenue runs at zero while its diplomacy and its mine tactics both hardened; America is being asked for ten-month tours with a quarter of its Reaper fleet gone and two-thirds of its interceptors spent. In both theaters the strikes set the news cycle and the supply line sets the trend. Ukraine's air defence is the binding constraint on its winter, and it is constrained by an American production schedule that does not clear until the 2030s.

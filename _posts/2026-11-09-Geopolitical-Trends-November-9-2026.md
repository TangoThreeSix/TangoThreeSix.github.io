Back in the r/CredibleDefense threads. Two things moved this week that the strike reports miss, and both are about price rather than position. Oil had its worst week since April 2020 — Brent down about 17 percent — because American voters, not negotiators, changed the odds. And Russia's answer to Ukraine's interceptor drones is now a decoy economy: fly cheap things that have to be shot down, so the expensive things get through. ### Iran — Diplomacy Repriced by a Midterm Result

Qatar's prime minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, met Trump at the White House on Monday, with Vice President Vance also scheduled to see him. Iran has put a two-day proposal through Qatari mediators on the table: on day one the US implements a set of measures, on day two Hormuz reopens. It builds on the seven-day plan Iran announced at the UN General Assembly in September, and it comes attached to seven demands — lift the blockade and the sanctions, draw down US forces, reinstate June's oil waivers, release frozen funds, recognise the Iran–Oman route through the strait, and end the war on all fronts.

Washington's read is not warm. Rubio called the demands "outrageous and ridiculous" and said talks are not at the verge of a breakthrough; Trump said renewed strikes were "highly unlikely" if a strong agreement emerged, while noting three carriers are in the region and 38 bridges are pre-marked as targets. Reports that CENTCOM is preparing to resume major operations rest on a single US official via Axios, and stay flagged.

Oil is the tell. Brent settled at $97.75 on Friday, a 16.9 percent weekly loss and the worst week since April 2020 — its steepest in absolute terms in LSEG data going back to 1988; WTI fell 13.2 percent. The move came from the Republican midterm losses and the hope of de-escalation, not a signed deal, and both benchmarks plunged more than 8 percent on Wednesday before clawing back. Prices were near $97 again on Monday. The physical market has not loosened: war-risk insurance still runs 3 to 10 percent of hull value against 0.25 percent before the war, and the IEA still calls this the largest supply disruption in the oil market's history.

### Iran — The Fleet, the Ledger, and the Inventory

USS Abraham Lincoln returned to San Diego on October 8 after 321 days, the second-longest post-Vietnam carrier deployment, five days short of Gerald R. Ford's 326. Consecutive days at sea is reported as 264 by The Maritime Executive and 286 by the Navy — the sources disagree, so treat the streak as a range. Rotation pressure has not eased: George Washington is in theatre and Theodore Roosevelt reached Singapore on November 6.

The ledger: 23 killed and 865 wounded as of October 29, on the Pentagon's count; a war cost of $47.1 billion through October 16, against CBO's $38 billion and the DoD inspector general's $33.4 billion through June — the figures diverge by accounting scope. Interceptor stocks are classified, so these are estimates, not facts: CSIS put Patriot expenditure at roughly 1,060 to 1,430 rounds, more than half the pre-war inventory, while CBO told Congress the war consumed half to two-thirds of combined Patriot, THAAD, SM-3 and SM-6 stocks, with a rebuild of at least five years. At least 45 MQ-9 Reapers, about a quarter of the fleet, are gone for more than $1.3 billion, sourced to three US officials. Iranian crude loadings are near zero and the rial sits at record lows. On inflation, the honest note: the IMF figure is reported at 68.9 percent in one relay and near 90 percent in another, against the 78.6 percent this brief carried last week — the 78.6 percent needs re-sourcing, and the range stands.

### Russian Drone Warfare — The Decoy Tax

September's launch volume was about 6,700 Shahed-type drones on the Air Force's reporting baseline, with a reconstruction of the daily reports putting the true total nearer 7,400; May's 8,161 remains the peak. October's General Staff figure was 6,473 deployed and 4,859 neutralised. The two methods are not directly comparable. The composition is shifting fast: jet-powered launches rose from roughly 350 in June to some 3,350 in September, an open-source estimate.

The year's largest energy strike came overnight on November 2 to 3: Zelensky's count of 289 drones, 17 ballistic missiles and 59 cruise missiles across nine oblasts. Ukraine intercepted 83 percent of the cruise missiles but only 17.65 percent of the ballistic and anti-ship types. The November 4 package was 310 drones; November 5 to 6 was 227, of them 86 jet-powered; November 7 to 8 was 164, of them 112 jet-powered.

Production stays a range. Ukrainian intelligence put jet Geran-4/5 output near 3,000 a month in August and raised it to about 4,000 in late October — an intelligence estimate, not a measured fact. CSIS cites Alabuga's capacity at roughly 3,500 jet plus 3,000 piston airframes a month, which is capacity, not output. The design story that matters is the decoy economy: the Parodiya carries no warhead and a Luneburg lens to mimic a Shahed's radar return, so every interceptor spent on it is one fewer for a real drone. A new shaped-charge warhead on the Geran-4 is built to slice bridge spans and power-line pylons. Ukraine's deputy defence minister says Russia now mounts jamming suppressors on Shaheds to blind pursuing FPVs — a single official, early. North Korean labour is present at scale but the count is disputed: Ukrainian intelligence says 12,000, NHK and Bloomberg say 25,000.

### Ukraine's Strikes — Refineries Again, and the Parts Chain

Overnight into November 9, Ukrainian drones set the Kirishi refinery in Leningrad region alight, Russia's second-largest at about 20 million tonnes a year and the main supplier for St. Petersburg. The regional governor put the downed-drone count at 81; LIGA's tally is 113. Earlier in the week, Saratov's refinery took at least its eighth strike of 2026 and Afipsky caught fire on November 6.

October's airbase work was satellite-confirmed: Khanskaya on October 4 (a Su-35 and a Su-34), Borisoglebsk on October 15 (at least four Su-34), Marinovka on October 29 (two Su-34). On refining capacity the claims diverge widely — Ukraine's MoD says 51 percent of capacity is disabled, Ukrainian intelligence says 15 refineries are shut and damage has reached $23.2 billion, the Wall Street Journal put the figure at up to 28 percent in June, and independent analysts sit at 20 to 40 percent. The honest presentation is the range.

### The Interceptor Economy — Cheap Drones, Contested Claims

The cost gap is the whole story. Ukrainian interceptors sit in a roughly $1,000 to $2,500 band; a Shahed costs an estimated $40,000 to $80,000 and a jet-powered one $100,000 to $150,000. Brave1 caps a launch at $20,000 to $25,000. Manufacturer numbers stay labelled as such: Wild Hornets puts the Sting at about $2,100 with an 80 to 90 percent hit rate and output above 10,000 a month, and SkyFall claims more than 5,500 Shahed kills and a 50,000-a-month capacity target. None of that is independently verified. Ukraine produced 100,000 interceptors in 2025 on its own count, delivers more than 1,500 a day to the front, and commissioned its first domestic jet interceptor, Alexa Spatium, in August; Serhii Sternenko says mass production against jet Shaheds will not arrive before the end of 2026. At sea, the Magura MV11 can carry up to 18 Sting interceptors in vertical cells (a manufacturer claim), and the Katran X1.2 carries up to 23 Dead Fly FPV interceptors.

### Connecting the Threads

Both wars are being repriced by politics and by the cost of the shot, not the size of the strike. Oil's worst week in five years came from a midterm result and the hope of a deal nobody has signed. Russia is spending airframes it does not need on targets it does not have to hit, to make Ukraine spend interceptors it cannot replace fast enough. And the American inventory picture, still classified, is the quiet constraint on both theatres: whatever happens in the Gulf, the production schedule that refills the cells for the Pacific does not clear this decade.

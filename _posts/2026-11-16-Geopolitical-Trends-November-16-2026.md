Back in the r/CredibleDefense threads. The balance of forces barely moved this week. The stories did. Three carriers arrived in the Gulf while the President said the war was effectively over, the Pentagon declared a historic victory while its own intelligence community leaked a 2,500-missile estimate, and Tehran lifted the range cap it had kept on its own missiles for years. In Ukraine, Russia stopped making drones faster and started making them into small cruise missiles.

---

### Iran — Three Carriers and a Range Cap

Theodore Roosevelt arrived on 15-16 November, giving the US three carrier strike groups in the Gulf with George H.W. Bush and George Washington, the second time since 2003. Neither the Pentagon nor CENTCOM has formally announced it; the news comes through anonymous officials.

On 10 November Hegseth called Iran's defence industrial base gone and the war a historic victory. Four days later US intelligence told the Wall Street Journal that Iran retains 1,500 to 2,500 ballistic missiles, at low confidence because launch readiness is unknown. Bloomberg has production near 50 a month, half the prewar rate. That is compatible with the 1,500 to 2,500 band, and 2,500 is its top, not a point estimate.

The bigger story is the range cap. On 10 November the IRGC confirmed it has lifted the self-imposed 2,000 km limit, citing a directive from Mojtaba Khamenei to respond without geographical limits, though spokesman Hossein Mohebbi did not say whether Iran already fields missiles beyond it. What changed is declared policy, and declared policy is what a programme gets built to. The Gulf was always inside 2,000 km; the difference now is Europe and the Indian Ocean.

### Iran — Who Pays, and When

The sustainment story does not photograph. Vice Chief of Naval Operations Adm. James Kilby said on 8 October that combat surge readiness rates are artificially high because the ships have not come home, and that the industrial base will not swallow the maintenance bill when they do.

On people, the most misreported item of the war: Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao's 22 September letter to Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand confirmed eight suicide attempts across the Lincoln strike group and none completed on board. One official called eight incredibly high; the Navy answers that eight in a group of 6,000 tracks its service-wide rate. The material conditions are not disputed: rationed meals, mould in showers, a clogged toilet bank, disrupted mail.

The magazine is the real constraint. CBO told Congress in September that the war consumed half to two-thirds of the combined Patriot, THAAD, SM-3 and SM-6 inventory, with a rebuild of at least five years. CSIS's 10 November update has two-thirds of prewar Patriot and half of THAAD. Deliveries run at roughly 15 Tomahawks and 20 Patriots a month, which makes the rebuild a decade's work, and at least 45 Reapers are gone. Congress spent the week assembling the bill: Bennet wants no package carrying war money, Kaine is drafting War Powers language for the 11 December CR, and the Senate still has not taken up July's $60 billion instruction.

### Oil — The Blockade Priced as Stable

Brent settled at $95.84 on Friday 13 November, up two cents, its second consecutive weekly loss at roughly 2 to 2.5 percent. One correction: the 6 November settle was $98.32, so the $97.75 carried here last week was an intraday print, not the close. Prices fell on IEA surplus signals and on Trump saying a deal is not necessary, even as tankers were reportedly mined. The market has begun pricing the blockade as the stable state.

The physical market has not loosened. War-risk insurance runs 3 to 8 percent of hull value against 0.25 percent before the war. Kpler recorded no crude loaded in September, the first zero month since 1979, and projects Iranian crude revenue hitting zero around mid-December. Inflation ran near 90 percent in September, superseding the 68.9 and 78.6 figures carried earlier.

### Russia — The Drone That Became a Missile

On 9 November Ukrainian military intelligence published a teardown of something it now calls a small cruise missile: the Geran-5 K5-K. Cruise speed is above 450 km/h, terminal around 1,000, ceiling 6 km. The specimen carried a 90 kg thermobaric warhead. The guidance is the part worth reading twice: satellite-aided inertial navigation, a 16-element anti-jam antenna HUR describes as a copy of a Chinese Harxon design, and an optical scene-matching correlator called Susanin on a Chinese microcomputer. Of 93 electronic components, 52 were American.

HUR's production figures are estimates, not counts: jet Geran-4/5 output around 4,000 a month by late October, up from about 3,000 in August. CSIS's cited Alabuga capacity of 3,500 jet airframes a month sits below the claimed output, so one figure is soft. Beskrestnov puts Geran-5 proper at about 700 a month.

### Russia — Cheaper Ways Through

Two adaptations landed this week and neither is a new airframe. On 12 November Beskrestnov reported, and ISW recorded, that jet Gerans now shut their engines immediately before impact, coasting in silently, and fly very low on approach, which targets acoustic detection and the mobile fire groups that are the cheapest layer of Ukrainian air defence. ISW assessed on 15 November that Russia has shifted launch geometry to Donetsk City and Hvardiiske so jet drones reach Zaporizhzhia and Dnipro on short tracks Ukrainian interceptors cannot contest.

Underneath both is the Starshield problem. Zelensky said on 12-13 November that a 161 km/h speed restriction on the satellite link is stopping Ukrainian interceptor drones from catching jet targets, and tied the substation strikes in seven oblasts to it. Ukrainian officials told Axios the disruption began on 10 November and all missions were failing by the 13th. SpaceX says nothing changed.

The official numbers: October saw 6,473 Shahed-type drones launched and 4,859 destroyed, 66 percent of the jet-powered ones and 89 percent of the piston ones. September's baseline per ISIS was 6,732, superseding the 6,700 and 7,400 pair carried here last week. My tally of the ten packages from 8 to 16 November is 1,547 drones, 807 of them jet. Against that, Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi said on 16 November that Russia could launch 6,500 jet-powered drones this month, twelve times August's 530, when the Air Force's own August figure was nearer 2,800. Treat it as a projection that does not reconcile with the government's data.

### Ukraine — Refineries, Ports, and One Bridge

The strike campaign had its busiest week of the war. On 11 November drones set the Ust-Luga oil and condensate port alight; Planet Labs imagery shows at least five burnt-out tanks, and S&P Global puts the port at 562,000 barrels a day of crude exports. The same day Omsk, Russia's largest refinery, burned for eight hours in a strike the Special Operations Forces confirmed, and the Buyan-M ship Naro-Fominsk was hit in the Gulf of Finland, confirmed by Finland and Estonia.

On 13 November the Volgograd refinery was struck and four workers killed. Over the weekend Nizhnekamsk in Tatarstan and the Akhtubinsk test airfield were hit as well. On 16 November the Kerch Bridge was struck. Vantor imagery provided to The War Zone shows visible deformation of the road-span arch, one lane reopened after roughly 17 hours. Peskov called the damage minor; the 15-degree tilt figure is a Russian Telegram claim.

On how much refining is down, the honest answer is a range. Zelensky said on 15 November that more than 55 percent of capacity has been neutralised, up from the defence ministry's 51 percent. Reuters reported the same week that strikes cut actual processing by up to 200,000 tonnes a day, about 30 percent, and the FT has 20 to 35 percent offline. Capacity claimed and throughput lost are different metrics, and only one shows up in export data. At the industrial end, UFORCE raised $700 million at a $6 billion valuation to build the Magura hulls in Britain.

### Connecting the Threads

Both wars are being decided by the cost of a shot, and both governments are telling the story they want rather than the story the numbers tell. Russia is spending airframes it does not need, on routes chosen to dodge a satellite speed cap, to make Ukraine spend interceptors it cannot build fast enough. America has three carriers on station and a magazine refilling at 20 interceptors a month. Iran has fewer launchers, no export revenue after mid-December, and a newly declared licence to build longer rockets. The cross-theatre link is the interceptor.

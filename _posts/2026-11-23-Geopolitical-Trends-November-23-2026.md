Back in the r/CredibleDefense threads. Two things happened this week that clarify where both wars are heading. In Washington, the Government Accountability Office confirmed there is no overarching plan to rebuild the munitions burned in the Iran war, even as shipping attacks surged again in the Gulf. In the Mediterranean, Ukrainian sea drones sank a Russian corvette docked in Benghazi, 2,400 kilometers from the front line. Geography is buying less safety than either side assumed.

---

### Iran — Blockade Pressure and a Missing Munitions Plan

The naval posture in CENTCOM stayed at three carrier strike groups this week following the arrival of USS Theodore Roosevelt on 15-16 November, yet the maritime front heated up anyway. UKMTO logged 19 shipping incidents in November as of 17 November, and three oil tankers were struck on 21 November. TankerTrackers reported an Iranian tanker loading crude via ship-to-ship transfer to test the U.S. blockade. Tehran called that report speculation on 23 November, while Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei vowed Iran would use all available means under UN Article 51 to confront the blockade.

The real story is in the Pentagon's sustainment ledger. On 19 November, the GAO publicly released report GAO-27-109420, warning that the Iran war has worsened persistent munitions shortages across the U.S. military. The audit found no overarching Defense Department plan linking production and funding to total warfighting requirements, and no designated office or leader directing recovery. Internal Pentagon figures obtained by Reuters in late October already put direct U.S. war costs above $47.1 billion through mid-October, including over $29 billion just to replace spent munitions. Meanwhile, the White House's June request for an $87.6 billion supplemental ($67.1 billion for defense) remains stalled in a lame-duck Congress.

That institutional gap matters because the magazine is thin. CSIS's 10 November update estimates the U.S. has expended roughly two-thirds of its pre-war Patriot interceptors, half of its THAAD interceptors, and a third of its Tomahawks, alongside 45 to 47 MQ-9 Reapers lost (roughly a quarter of the fleet). Earlier August reporting in CNN put THAAD expenditure even higher, near 80 percent, and Patriot near half. Deliveries still crawl at roughly 20 Patriots a month, 15 Tomahawks a month, and 96 THAAD interceptors a year. RUSI notes the Navy has also stretched carrier and amphibious cycles to breaking points, leaving Indo-Pacific deterrence exposed while the Pentagon tries to sustain a three-carrier presence in the Gulf.

Iran's economic base is absorbing heavy damage under the U.S. naval blockade, though its surviving military capacity remains contested. The IMF's October World Economic Outlook downgraded Iran's 2026 real GDP forecast to a 7.6 percent contraction, with a 68.9 percent annual average inflation forecast. Official Statistical Center of Iran data showed point-to-point inflation running near 88 percent in June and July, while Kpler recorded zero Iranian crude loadings in September. On missile stocks, assessments diverge: FDD estimates Iran lost a third to half of its missiles and half its launchers, whereas a leaked U.S. intelligence estimate cited by the Wall Street Journal put retained stocks at 1,500 to 2,500 missiles at low confidence because launch readiness is unknown.

### Oil — Crude Loosens, Diesel Doesn't

The IEA's 13 November Oil Market Report and 19 November World Energy Outlook put hard numbers on the Hormuz disruption. Cumulative Gulf losses through October reached 2.8 billion barrels of oil and 60 billion cubic meters of natural gas, and IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol stated that confidence in Hormuz as a reliable corridor has been crushed.

Yet crude prices softened further this week. Under U.S. naval escorts, Gulf oil exports rebounded to 18.4 million barrels a day in October, still 26 percent below pre-war levels, pushing global supply back toward a fourth-quarter surplus. North Sea Dated crude fell to around $93 a barrel in mid-November, down from $144 in May and Friday 13 November's Brent close of $95.84. The catch is refined fuel. Gulf exports of refined products and LPG remain near 50 percent of pre-war volume, keeping European diesel near $180 a barrel, still 77 percent above pre-war prices.

### Russia — Saturation at Scale, and a Ballistic Gap

Russia's 19-20 November overnight barrage showed both the scale of its winter strike campaign and Ukraine's sharpest air defense vulnerability. Moscow launched 404 drones (including 123 jet-powered units), 27 ballistic missiles, and 84 cruise missiles at energy grids across 15 oblasts and Kyiv's Dnipro River bridges. Polish and NATO forces tracked Russian missiles and drones entering Polish airspace 35 kilometers from the Coalition of the Willing's Exercise Koalicja in Nowa Deba.

The interception breakdown tells the story. Ukrainian air defenses downed roughly 94 percent of the cruise missiles and drones in the 19-20 November attack, but intercepted only 3 of 27 ballistic missiles, about 11 percent. That gap reflects the acute shortage of Patriot PAC-3 interceptors, directly linking Middle Eastern munition burn rates to Ukrainian skies.

On the drone manufacturing side, estimates vary across sources. Ukrainian military intelligence (DIU/HUR) assesses Russia scaled jet-powered Geran-4 and Geran-5 output from roughly 3,000 a month in August to up to 4,000 a month by October, whereas Russian defense reporting in Voennoedelo put September jet output closer to 2,000 a month. Debris forensics indicate these drones rely on Chinese Telefly TF-TJ2000A turbojets assembled at Alabuga rather than a fully indigenous Russian engine. Foreign labor at Alabuga is also disputed in scale: DIU/HUR estimates roughly 12,000 North Korean workers, while the Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team reported up to 25,000, alongside an August HUR assessment of 400 North Korean drone operators deployed near the front.

Ukraine's counter-drone layer is adapting, even under the 161 km/h Starshield speed restriction reported by Kyiv earlier this month. On 23 November, German-Ukrainian joint venture Quantum WIY released combat video claiming its upgraded SPYS interceptor solo-destroyed a Geran-5 K5-K flying above 450 km/h, a manufacturer claim backed by footage. Sweden's Nordic Air Defence also unveiled its autonomous K200 interceptor on 20 November for Ukraine trials.

### Ukraine — From the Kerch Bridge to Benghazi

Follow-on details from last week's 16-17 November Kerch Bridge strike confirmed a seven-stage operation using 11 domestic systems, including Palianytsia, Ruta, Neptune, guided FP-5 Flamingos, and the combat debut of the jet-powered An-197 Liutyi. A second Neptune strike hit the bridge again overnight on 18-19 November. Road traffic was severed while single-track rail service resumed.

Ukraine's deep-strike campaign against Russian energy also kept pace. Between 13 and 21 November, Ukrainian drones hit the Ilsky and Afipsky refineries, facilities in Salavat (about 1,500 km out) and Ufa, and the Kaleikino-1 Druzhba pipeline hub in Tatarstan. Bloomberg counted 13 Russian refineries attacked in November through 17 November. Exact offline refining capacity remains a wide range because Moscow stopped publishing data: open-source and energy estimates run from 20 percent to 45 percent at peak, compared with 42 to 55 percent claimed by Ukrainian officials.

The week's most striking operational reach happened far from the Black Sea. On 22 November, Ukraine's SBU launched Sea Baby naval drones from a covert mother ship roughly 2,400 kilometers from Ukraine, sinking Russia's modern Steregushchiy-class corvette Merkury (and its embarked Ka-27 helicopter) at Benghazi, Libya, while also striking the sanctioned cargo ship MV Angara. Vantor satellite imagery confirmed Merkury capsized at port. Kyiv stated seven drones were deployed; anonymous Libyan military sources told reporters over 20 drone boats were intercepted offshore, leaving the exact launch count unresolved.

---

### Connecting the Threads

Both conflicts are converging on the same constraint: interceptor math and long-range maritime vulnerability. The U.S. is burning through Patriot and THAAD stocks in the Middle East without a funded or unified Pentagon recovery plan, and Ukraine is feeling that exact scarcity when ballistic salvos arrive. At the same time, low-cost unmanned systems are erasing safe rear areas, whether in the Strait of Hormuz or a Libyan port 2,400 kilometers from Crimea.
